# Mushi Lantern — Adrift VR jam charter and prototype plan

Working title. A small Mushishi fan-game concept: shepherd scattered mushi through a dark grove with a shuttered, color-filtered lantern, returning them to a migration flowing across the night sky.

## State

**Planning only, captured from the user's design on 2026-09-21.** No repository has been created or inspected for this project, no implementation has been run, and neither gameplay feel nor visual/headset feasibility has been validated. This note is a handoff for local Codex, not evidence that the prototype exists.

Constraints: one-week Godot VR jam; theme “adrift”; PC-first, no Quest requirement; singleplayer baseline; the point should be apparent within 5–10 minutes, with optional continued exploration. User is experienced with Godot/VR basics. Desktop WASD/mouselook is the preferred first testing surface. Actual jam start/deadline dates, local project path, engine version, and renderer are not yet recorded.

**User-selected direction:** lightweight alife/boids, animalistic/primordial mushi rather than human ghosts, a moonless forest, virtual dark adaptation, a swinging lantern hanging from a staff, attraction/calming and repulsion/energizing filters, spatialized creature sounds, a central campfire and guide, a sky-stream reveal and visible return-to-migration payoff. The guide demonstrates the tools, lends the lantern, counts successful returns, and congratulates the player at a modest threshold without ending free exploration.

**Provisional design choices below are tunable proposals, not user-approved constants.** In particular, blue = attract/calm and orange = repel/energize is a convenient initial mapping. The colors are magical affordances, not a model of physiological wavelength sensitivity. Do not infer engine versions, paths, or implementation state from another VR project.

## Charter

### Experience to preserve

You are volunteering to help strays rejoin a migration, not collecting pets or defeating ghosts. Most of the grove looks empty under ordinary light. Closing the lantern changes what you can perceive: nearby mushi emerge first, then distant groups, then an enormous moving river across the sky. You cannot see the forest and the spirit world equally well at once.

The recurring loop is: orient with ordinary light; pause and shade it to notice/listen; choose a group; coax or push the group around terrain; deliver it to the clearing; watch it rise and disappear into the larger movement overhead. The campfire is the recurring orientation point, not a large map-wide tractor beam.

The desired handling is persuasion rather than cursor magnetism. Mushi respond with inertia and local social behavior, while remaining sufficiently readable that a player can deliberately gather, lead, turn, and release them. The staff lets the player place the influence somewhere other than their own body. Its swing makes the lantern visibly physical; it must not make steering unpredictably inaccurate.

### Minimum complete game

One compact authored grove, one behaviorally unified mushi species, one staff/lantern, a shutter and two filters, one central return point, one simple guide, a sky-stream, a short tutorial, and a non-blocking completion acknowledgment. Shape, pulse, size, and sound variation can create variety without additional species rules.

No combat, health, timer pressure, mandatory 100% hunt, inventory management, or failure by permanently losing individual mushi. No ocean/space setting: the stream belongs to the sky above a forest, not a space-travel game.

Non-goals for the first prototype: procedural forest generation, GPU boids, general ecosystem simulation, reproduction/evolution, emergent composite creatures, a familiar, networking, full spectral optics, realistic retinal adaptation, arbitrary rigid-body creature navigation, or a fully animated/voiced NPC. Attractive systems are not automatically jam requirements.

## 1. M0 — test the herding, without darkness

### Deliverable

A standalone first-person 3D greybox with ground-constrained mushi, an offset controllable light source, a goal circle, two or three trunk-shaped obstacles, and a small live-tuning/debug panel. Mushi are always visible. A top-down diagnostic camera is useful, but first-person play is the actual test.

Start with three groups of eight mushi, with an option to reset to a tiny three-creature fixture. Compare larger populations only after the small one is readable. Use a finite population: no background spawning that disguises lost agents or makes scoring ambiguous.

**Initial controls:** WASD and mouselook; filter actions for clear/blue/orange; a shutter toggle plus continuous openness adjustment; reset, pause, and debug toggles. Use a broad, slightly downward-facing test lamp held at an offset from the camera. Mouse aiming or a modifier for independent lamp placement can be added if the fixed offset conceals the value of lateral placement. Do not spend M0 building a desktop hand-manipulation system or staff physics.

Display the active mode with an icon/text as well as color. The panel exposes a few meaningful controls, saves named presets, and restores the seed and layout. Keep detailed coefficients in a resource/config rather than presenting fifty equally prominent sliders.

### Three comparison presets, not an open-ended rule search

1. **Plain boids plus seek/flee:** separation, alignment, cohesion, gentle wandering, and immediate lantern response. This is the baseline.
2. **The same plus arousal memory:** light gradually changes activity and noise; the response persists briefly after the beam moves away. This is the main hypothesis.
3. **Independent seekers control:** disable social terms but retain the same light behavior. This checks whether flocking contributes useful coordination or merely adds confusing noise.

Use the same layouts/seeds for comparisons, and change one or two dimensions at a time. Begin with attraction only, then add repulsion, then arousal. Save the simple versions rather than overwriting them as features accumulate.

### Human test situations

**Gather and carry:** approach three strays, settle them into a group, then transport them roughly 8–10 meters. Can the player predict when movement is too fast? Does placing the light differently change the result?

**Turn and release:** guide a group around a trunk and toward the goal, then move to its rear/flank and use orange to nudge it in. Compare with simply leading it all the way using blue. Do not secretly forbid the simpler solution just to force a filter switch.

**Recover a split:** allow a few agents to separate at a fork. Can the player recover them without the main group immediately scattering? Test whether a stationary calming source is a useful temporary resting place.

M0 succeeds when gathering, turning, and releasing involve legible choices and are pleasant to repeat for a few minutes. It is not enough that an automated script reaches the goal. Warning signs are mindless vacuuming with blue, orange causing unrecoverable scatter, endlessly waiting for slow agents, and a flock that behaves like an unsteerable cloud.

**Decision after M0:** keep the simplest enjoyable preset. If the motion is pleasant but challenge is thin, a short observational exploration game may already be sufficient; do not automatically add species or a composite-creature system. If neither the control nor observation is engaging, change the tool/response before decorating the forest.

## 2. Simulation and light-response contract

### Ground-constrained motion

Simulate horizontal position/velocity in XZ; derive height from a simple ground surface plus a small visual bob. Start flat. Ground projection and obstacle avoidance can follow for mild slopes. The ascending departure sequence is a separate lifecycle, not a reason to make the entire simulation freely 3D.

Use a fixed simulation step, stable IDs, a seeded random generator, and snapshot/two-buffer neighbor updates so iteration order does not accidentally become a flocking rule. Interpolate visual movement between simulation updates. Reproducible resets on the same build are enough; do not claim cross-platform deterministic physics.

Start with a straightforward CPU neighbor loop and simple meshes in typed GDScript, unless the local project already has a better established convention. Measure before adding a spatial grid, native extension, or MultiMesh. M0 needs tens of agents, not a scalable simulation framework.

The baseline acceleration combines separation, alignment, cohesion, persistent wander, obstacle avoidance, and lantern steering. Cap acceleration and speed. Give collision avoidance priority so an attractive source behind a trunk cannot pull agents through it. Simple circle/capsule obstacles and a bounded play area are enough initially. Do not hide persistent navigation failures with silent teleportation.

Reynolds' original boids model is already three-dimensional and uses the familiar three local rules; ground confinement is a deliberate simplification here, not a missing form of boids. His steering treatment also distinguishes seek/flee from arrival, useful for avoiding endless orbiting around the lantern. [R1, R2]

### One lantern state, three separately tunable outputs

Represent the lantern by its actual transform, filter mode, shutter openness, and emitter profile. That state drives:

- **Forest illumination:** rendered light, range, intensity, color, and broad aperture/cookie shape.
- **Mushi stimulus:** the field sampled by agents, independent of their visibility to the player.
- **Player exposure:** input to virtual adaptation, independent of whether the agents are simulated.

Do not infer gameplay behavior from an RGB value or final screen brightness. Blue mushrooms, a blue filter, and a warm campfire have explicit semantic profiles. The campfire is not an orange repulsor merely because it looks orange.

| Mode | Forest illumination | Mushi stimulus | Initial adaptation target away from fire |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Clear/open | Strong and useful for navigation | Neutral initially | Low |
| Blue | Dim, still enough nearby light for careful movement | Attract; reduce arousal/wandering; settle near source | Fairly high |
| Orange | Dim | Repel; increase arousal/speed/noise, within limits | Medium-high |
| Shutter closed | Minimal leakage | None | Highest |

The attraction and repulsion filters need not have equal ranges or intensities. Avoid making filtered light so useful for terrain and spirit visibility that clear light and full darkness are both redundant.

### Field sampling

For each source, use a smooth bounded distance falloff multiplied by aperture/angular coverage, mode strength, and an occlusion term:

```text
stimulus(p) = mode_strength * aperture_gain * radial_falloff(distance)
              * angular_mask(source_local_direction) * line_of_sight
```

Sample at the mushi's rendered body height, using the lamp position/orientation rather than the player head or staff handle. Apply seek/flee steering in XZ. This can be field-gated seek/flee; it need not be the exact gradient of a physically accurate illumination field.

Use a soft edge so a swinging lamp does not switch agents abruptly between fully affected and unaffected. Add short response smoothing/hysteresis. Hard trunks/rocks occlude the field; tiny decorative grass need not. Start with cheap analytic occlusion matching M0 obstacles; use selected physics ray tests later if needed. Do not read GPU light buffers back to run the simulation.

Share the broad angular/range parameters between the gameplay field and visible light. A debug overlay should reveal whether the footprint and the reacting agents agree. Decorative cookie slats may be simplified, but creatures visibly behind a large shade or trunk should not behave as if fully lit.

### Blue: arrival without immobilization

Seek the light's ground footprint and slow near it, retaining enough separation to form a small loose cluster instead of occupying a single point. Lower wandering/agitation in blue. **Do not globally reduce maximum speed to almost zero whenever blue touches an agent:** that makes calming incompatible with leading.

When a settled creature falls behind a moving lantern, distance-to-target must restore enough locomotion to catch up. A distance-dependent arrival speed plus a nonzero pursuit speed is sufficient. It is acceptable to keep resting visually distinct from following without adding another large behavior tree.

A blue luminous mushroom can reuse the same profile as a stationary rest attractor. Introduce this only after baseline leading works. It should create a useful staging point or a mild distraction, not make groups permanently impossible to extract. Establish whether the nearby moving lantern can win the competition or whether a short orange pulse is helpful.

### Orange: pressure, not an explosion

Steer away from the source; position of the source relative to the flock determines the push. Increase activity and wandering modestly, keeping enough cohesion that the player can push a group rather than destroy it. Cap combined forces and taper them near the edge.

Use temporally correlated wandering, not a fresh unrelated direction every render frame. Orange should feel like a local wake-up/disturbance. If the only way to use it is repeated whole-flock recovery, reduce noise before inventing recovery mechanics.

### Arousal memory: the only extra alife variable initially

An optional scalar `e` per mushi relaxes toward a baseline and is driven downward by blue and upward by orange. Use it to modulate preferred speed, wander, pulse, and perhaps audio activity. Smooth changes over time. This makes a moving patch of light leave a brief behavioral trace and prevents rapid shutter toggles from producing digital-looking responses.

Do not add energy budgets, metabolism, fear, hunger, species affinities, or social-role graphs until a concrete test identifies a need. Small fixed per-agent variation can be added after control readability is established.

### Goal lifecycle and accounting

The campfire has a clearly bounded **return/launch region around it**, not a requirement to steer creatures into burning geometry. Locally funnel accepted agents inward and upward.

```text
ACTIVE -> COMMITTED -> ASCENDING -> RELEASED
```

Commit after crossing the return region, optionally with a short dwell to reject incidental edge touches. Increment score exactly once at commitment; remove the agent from active flocking and lantern response. Ascending agents must also stop contributing to active neighbor forces, or the ground flock may follow them for the wrong reason. Move them onto a authored upward curve that visually merges into the stream. Once committed, no later repulsion or loss of visibility can undo progress.

No long-range goal attraction: an unattended grove should not collect itself. The enormous sky migration is decorative/path-driven and does not need to be the same simulated population. Preserve a believable visual handoff, not global biological conservation across thousands of particles.

## 3. M1 — visibility, adaptation, and the sky reveal

### Keep spirit sensitivity explicit

Implement a local-player adaptation value `A` in [0,1]. Begin with an authored exposure estimate from shutter/filter state and nearby campfire exposure. A later refinement can account for looking toward a bright source or nearby illuminated surfaces. Full screen-luminance analysis is not required.

A useful frame-rate-independent update is:

```text
A = target_A + (A - target_A) * exp(-dt / tau)
tau = desired_time_to_90_percent / ln(10)
```

Choose separate recovery and loss times. Initial **testing values**, not established results: 4–8 seconds to recover 90% of the difference in darkness; 0.5–1.5 seconds to lose 90% under strong clear light. Offer slower/faster settings for comparison. Recovery should be noticeable but not repeatedly stall play.

An initial target table could be closed 1.0, clear 0.1, blue 0.8, orange 0.65, modified smoothly by aperture and fire exposure. These are game coefficients, not actual visual sensitivity units. Avoid pure binary modes: the useful resource is recent exposure, not just the currently selected filter.

### Separate ground visibility from sky visibility

For local mushi, expand the visible radius with adaptation, retaining a small close-range floor:

```text
visible_radius = lerp(near_radius, adapted_radius, response_curve(A))
visibility = 1 - smoothstep(visible_radius - fade_width, visible_radius, distance)
```

Start testing around a 1–2 meter close-range floor and 12–18 meters fully adapted, scaled to the actual grove. A narrow close-range silhouette keeps adjusting the tool possible while distant groups vanish. Fade opacity/emission together as appropriate; do not leave an unexplained bloom blob after the body disappears. Invisible agents continue simulating and can still make sounds.

The stars and sky-stream use their own adaptation curve, not this ground-agent distance cutoff. Nearby mushi become apparent before the full sky reveal. Treat the stream as world-anchored geometry/sky content, not a head-locked overlay. Ensure it remains visible through the canopy from the starting clearing.

Use a shared head-center adaptation/distance reference for both eyes so one eye does not cross a visibility threshold independently of the other. The scene still renders naturally in stereo. Do not let mushi emission, their bloom, or the newly visible sky reduce `A` in a feedback loop.

Prefer material/sky parameters to a global brightness ramp as the core effect. Ordinary exposure adjustments may be supporting art direction, but increasing all scene brightness alone does not implement selective mushi vision. Godot's automatic exposure is a separate renderer feature, documented as Forward+-only in the reviewed documentation. [R4]

### The campfire conflict must be resolved deliberately

The starting fire, the returning flock, and the best sky-viewing point occupy the same place. Unqualified “bright light destroys all adaptation” would invalidate both the tutorial and every delivery payoff.

Initial solution: a small low/screened fire whose strongest ordinary-light exposure is local and view-dependent. The player can stand by the guide, look out/up, and adapt; looking down into the fire has a larger effect. The fire remains a compact visible landmark at distance, not a floodlight over the grove. The fictional launch region can grant a minimum visibility to committed/ascending mushi so the return is always readable.

Make that exception authored and coherent, not an accidental side effect of ignoring all fire light. The guide can explicitly stand on the dark side of the fire and shade the lantern before the reveal. Avoid a tutorial-only cheat that the player cannot reproduce afterward.

### Visibility lab acceptance

With the same camera position, capture clear-light, early recovery, fully adapted, blue, and orange states. Keep a debug override for fully visible mushi and forced `A`.

The player should be able to navigate with clear light, discover meaningfully more in darkness, maintain useful but incomplete awareness with filters, hear a nearby hidden group, and see every successful ascent. Check that a normal screenshot looking “too dark” is not mistaken for an acceptable headset result, or vice versa. The player must never need actual biological dark adaptation or an unlit physical room for the mechanic to function.

Keep modest terrain silhouettes and an optional minimum-visibility/calibration setting. No deceptive holes or collision traps in darkness. Color filters also differ in symbols, sounds, and physical detents.

## 4. M2 — VR staff and lantern

Swap the desktop driver for tracked hands while preserving the same lantern state and simulation. No rewrite of behavior around XR input nodes.

Use a tracked/kinematic staff and one hanging lantern with a damped bob. A single constrained mass, analytic pendulum, or simple jointed rigid body is enough; do not build a general rope system. Test whether swing makes placement satisfying before increasing realism. Keep a non-swinging comparison setting.

The other hand can steady the lantern frame, rotate/open the blind, and select one filter at a time. Generous grab zones, detents, and a temporary increase in damping while adjusting are preferable to making the player chase a moving tiny switch. Blinds and filters should remain usable for seated/limited-reach play. Provide simple button equivalents as a fallback without replacing the physical interaction.

The lamp transform drives both the visible cookie and the behavioral footprint. It must not lag in one system but not the other. The tracked camera is never attached to or moved by the pendulum. The staff/lantern should not physically launch the player on collision. On tracking loss, recenter, or teleport, reset or rebase pendulum velocities rather than generating an enormous impulse. Provide an obvious recall/dock/reset action for a dropped tool.

Retain a stable horizon, no forced head animation, and no head bob. Support the user's usual locomotion setup; smooth movement and snap turning are sufficient initially, with teleport worth keeping if already available. Teleporting should not teleport followers. Record the actual target refresh/frame budget on the chosen headset rather than assuming a desktop FPS result proves VR comfort.

**VR gate:** can the player place the light at a flank, lead a small group, bring it near the free hand, switch modes, and resume without fighting the tool? Check both eyes for the visibility fade, projected pattern, glow, and sky. Headset comfort and handling require a human trial, not just an XR startup log.

## 5. M3 — a complete short grove

### Layout and encounters

Author a small loop with a central clearing and three nearby gathering pockets. Keep the fire or a recoverable sound/landmark bearing available; getting geographically lost is not the main challenge. Begin with a flat or gently sloped floor and sparse trunks rather than dense undergrowth with complicated collision.

Reuse the same species and tool rules across situations: an easy exposed cluster, a mushroom resting patch, and a route around a trunk/constriction that rewards placing the lamp to one side. A sound-led hidden pocket is useful if the visibility mechanic is already legible. Not every situation needs a bespoke puzzle.

Start with a threshold around two-thirds of the non-tutorial population, adjustable after playtests. The first acknowledgment should land within the overall 5–10-minute experience, not require finding every last creature. Do not chase a completion-time target by making the simulation silently auto-complete.

### Tutorial beat sheet

1. Player starts by the campfire. A simple guide holds the lit lantern on its staff, welcomes the volunteer, and explains that scattered mushi need help rejoining the migration.
2. Guide closes/shades the lantern. During a brief textbox/optional narration, nearby mushi gradually become visible; the sky-stream follows. Cue looking upward but never rotate the player's head.
3. Guide opens the blue filter and demonstrates one nearby mushi approaching/settling.
4. Guide moves the orange-filtered source behind the mushi, nudging it into the launch region. The creature rises and visually joins the stream.
5. Guide hands over the staff and remains by the fire. Player explores freely. Keep a compact reminder of shutter/filter affordances available.
6. Guide's sensor/counter records returns. At the threshold, a short congratulation and stronger group departure/audio acknowledgment communicate completion. The player keeps control and may continue or leave.

Use a scripted tutorial actor/controlled fixture if needed for reliable presentation, but demonstrate the same rules and lamp placement used in play. Keep tutorial accounting separate so a demonstration cannot repeatedly inflate the score. Tutorial skip/reset should leave the tool, adaptation, population, and score in a valid state.

No full character animation pipeline is needed: a silhouette/simple figure, limited gestures, readable text, and a good handoff are enough. Voice is optional. Preserve the fan-game intention without making imported anime clips, soundtrack, or elaborate character recreation dependencies; record asset provenance and jam submission requirements before packaging.

### Audio

Give nearby mushi restrained spatialized calls or a soft textural rustle. Visibility does not gate sound: the player can hear a hidden group while using clear light. Footsteps naturally mask delicate cues, so stopping to listen has value; do not secretly freeze the creatures or enable a separate scanner just because the player stops.

Budget a small pool of nearby voices/events rather than an always-on loop for every boid. Use distance and a little selection hysteresis so sources do not pop around every frame. Preserve location information; do not average a widely separated group into a misleading center. A faint fire crackle can aid orientation, but must not drown every nearby mushi cue.

Calming/energizing can change pulse/call rate modestly. Separate quiet creature discovery, lantern/filter feedback, and the collective ascent payoff in the mix. No jump scare or pursuing monster is required.

## 6. Godot implementation shape and rendering checks

Keep this small. A scene/controller for the lab, a flock simulation script, a light-profile resource, a lantern driver, an adaptation component, and a goal/stream controller are sufficient boundaries. These can be ordinary nodes/scripts; do not build an ECS, generic signal bus, or plugin framework to connect them.

Suggested responsibilities, not a mandatory directory hierarchy:

```text
FlockSimulation       state arrays, neighbor update, movement, lifecycle IDs
LightField            source transform/profile, field query, debug footprint
Lantern               shutter/filter state; visual emitter; desktop or XR driver
Adaptation            local viewer sensitivity and visibility parameters
ReturnPoint           one-time commitment/score, ascent handoff
SkyStream             decorative migration, local arrival blending
PrototypePanel        presets, seed/reset, a few sliders, inspect/pause
```

Use a short list of light sources so a static mushroom or a second test lamp does not require duplicating the behavior code. This is not authorization to add multiplayer infrastructure.

At project pickup, inspect the installed Godot version and known working XR setup; choose and record the renderer. The reviewed Godot 4.7 XR guide recommends Mobile for desktop VR and says Forward+ also runs XR but is less optimized. PC-first does not inherently imply Forward+. Treat this as guidance to verify on the actual installed build. [R3]

A useful first rendering probe is one spotlight, an occluding trunk, a procedural/slatted projector texture, a few emissive creatures, and the adaptation fade. Godot's documented `Light3D.light_projector` supports Mobile and Forward+, not Compatibility, and requires `shadow_enabled`. Verify the shadow/projector combination immediately rather than assuming a decorative texture will work with shadows disabled. [R5]

Start with a single main shadowed lantern light and a modest fire. Do not give every luminous creature a dynamic light. Emissive material visibility and selective reveal should work without bloom; add glow only after checking the chosen renderer and both-eye result. Avoid depending on volumetric fog, temporal trails, or screen-space tricks for the core mechanic. Consider MultiMesh only if profiling identifies submission overhead; a large decorative stream may benefit sooner than a few dozen interactive mushi. [R6]

For shared material parameters, keep adaptation local to the current viewer. One local view may use shared/global parameters conveniently, but future multiple local viewports would need independent material/view state. Do not network a single global adaptation value for all players.

### Small amount of instrumentation, not a research platform

For a run record: seed, preset, agent count, elapsed time, returns, time in each lantern mode, and optional notes. Save a short clip/screenshot set of the three M0 situations. A debug-selected agent can show active forces, exposure/arousal, and lifecycle state.

Add a few focused automated checks: finite positions/speeds; bounded acceleration; shutter-closed field is zero; correct attraction/repulsion sign in an isolated fixture; broad occluder blocks stimulus; blue near-source settling still allows pursuit when the target moves; frame-rate-independent adaptation; score increments once; reset restores the initial population; ascending agents no longer influence ground neighbors.

An unattended/no-lantern run is a useful negative control for automatic goal collection. Use the same seeds to compare presets, but don't demand huge Monte Carlo studies, model fitting, or cross-platform lockstep for a one-week game. Test logs establish correctness boundaries, not fun, sound quality, or comfort.

These are scope budgets, not scheduled jobs or known jam dates. Rebalance toward the actual bottleneck.

**Cut order if behind:** multiplayer/familiar/composites first; then narration and character animation; then elaborate cookie mechanics/volumetrics; then encounter count. Simplify the pendulum or physical filter interface if needed. Preserve the hand-held light, understandable herding, adaptation/sky reveal, and a visible successful return. Those are the game's identity.

If attraction alone is enjoyable, shipping it with a modest optional repulsion role is better than forced gadget alternation. If a detailed arousal model makes movement opaque, return to plain boids with a small response delay. If filtering and adaptation produce constant idle waiting, shorten recovery and raise filtered sensitivity before adding another tool.

## 8. Stretch features, explicitly deferred

**Co-op:** multiple lanterns are simple at the interaction-model level, but authority/replication, ownership, per-view adaptation, and conflicting light fields are additional work. If the singleplayer build is complete, use a host-authoritative flock and score, send lamp states/poses, and interpolate remote presentation. Each player computes their own adaptation from nearby relevant sources. Define how overlapping attraction/repulsion caps or combines. Test whether one player's clear light unintentionally ruins everyone's visibility; choose a deliberate proximity/own-lantern rule. No co-op-only obstacle.

**Composite creatures:** only pursue after the ordinary herd is pleasant and a concrete visual payoff is desired. First try an authored formation in the ascent, or a cosmetic shape that appears at high local density. Do not turn a jam herding prototype into open-ended emergent morphology research.

**Additional ecology:** a second mushroom behavior or a distant mature form is lower scope than a new creature species. A familiar is currently outside the chosen lantern-only baseline.
